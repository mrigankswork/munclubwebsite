01

Formal Debate — General Speaker's List

The General Speaker's List (GSL) is the main form of debate in a formal session. Nations will be recognized by the Executive Board to speak. While the GSL is a general form of speech, a few key points must be kept in mind:

  • "General" does not mean a typical speech — your address must be substantive and targeted to the agenda.
  • Forming a good argument is very important — structure your points logically and back them with evidence.
  • Always ask questions — engaging with other delegates demonstrates active participation and analytical thinking.

After completing a GSL speech, if time remains, a delegate can yield their remaining time in one of three ways:

  • Yield to another delegate
  • Yield to the Executive Board
  • Yield to Points of Information
02

Informal Debate

Informal debate takes two forms — Moderated Caucuses and Unmoderated Caucuses. Each serves a distinct purpose in advancing committee work.

Moderated Caucus
  • Executive Board moderates the discussion
  • Individual speaking time is required
  • Delegates must speak in the order recognized by the E.B.
  • Use this time to make effective, focused speeches
Unmoderated Caucus
  • Executive Board doesn't moderate
  • Only total time is required
  • Delegates are free to use time as they wish
  • Use this time to lobby people to your side
03

Roll Call & Motion to Set Up GSL

At the beginning of each session, the Executive Board will conduct the Roll Call to identify which States are present and which are not.

There are two appropriate responses when your State is called:

  • "Present" — you retain the right to abstain on substantive votes.
  • "Present and Voting" — you are not permitted to abstain on any substantive vote while the committee is in session. You must vote strictly in favour or in opposition to any motion before the committee.

This is followed by a Motion to set up the General Speaker's List, which allows the committee to move into debating inside a formal session.

Motion to Set Speaker's Time of GSL

"The delegate of [Country] would like to set the individual Speaker's time of the GSL to 60 seconds."

To pass, the vote requires a Simple Majority.

04

Raising a Motion

A motion is made to suspend formal debate for the purpose of an Unmoderated Caucus or a Moderated Caucus. When raising a motion, the delegate should specify a certain amount of time and the topic (only in case of a Moderated Caucus).

For an Unmoderated Caucus

"With the prior permission of the Executive Board, the delegate of [Country] would like to suspend formal debate and move into an Unmoderated Caucus for the total time of ten minutes."

For a Moderated Caucus

"With the prior permission of the Executive Board, the delegate of [Country] would like to suspend formal debate and move into a Moderated Caucus on the topic [Topic] with individual speaking time being 60 seconds and total time being 20 minutes."

To pass, the vote requires a Simple Majority.

05

Parliamentary Points

I
Point of Inquiry
When the floor is open, a delegate may move for a Point of Inquiry to ask the chairperson a question regarding the Rules of Procedure.
II
Point of Personal Privilege
A delegate may raise this point to inform the chairperson of a physical discomfort, such as inability to hear another delegate's speech.
III
Point of Information
After a delegate has given a speech in formal debate and yields to Points of Information, another delegate may raise a question concerning the speech.
IV
Point of Order
This is raised when a factual error is made, either by a delegate or even by the Executive Board. The E.B. may ask the delegate to provide proof to confirm the correct fact.
06

Chits

Chits are written notes passed during committee sessions. They can be of three types:

  • Delegate to Delegate — direct communication between two delegates.
  • Delegate to Delegate (Via E.B.) — communication that passes through the Executive Board for review.
  • Delegate to E.B. (Substantive Chits) — chits addressed directly to the Executive Board on substantive matters.
Important

Use chits effectively as they can cause you to win and/or lose by very small margins. Strategic communication through chits is a critical component of MUN diplomacy.

07

Proofs in Committee

When making claims or countering arguments in committee, delegates must cite valid sources. The following are considered acceptable sources of proof:

  • Reuters — a globally trusted news agency.
  • Government news sources — official publications from national governments.
  • Reports from International Organizations — such as the UN, WHO, IMF, World Bank, etc.
Note

Reports from NGOs are not considered valid sources of proof in most MUN committees.

08

How to Give an Effective Speech

A good speech has four major elements. Mastering these will significantly improve your committee performance:

1
Explanation of the problem — clearly define the issue at hand and its scope.
2
A solution to counter the problem — propose actionable, realistic solutions.
3
Relevance to the agenda — explain why your speech matters in the context of the committee's agenda.
4
Questions and analysis — critically engage with other delegates' speeches and arguments.
09

How to Research for an MUN

Effective research also requires four major elements. Strong preparation is the foundation of every successful MUN experience:

1
Understand why the problem is happening — dig deep into root causes, not just symptoms.
2
Develop new, innovative solutions — go beyond existing proposals and craft original approaches.
3
Relevance to the agenda — ensure your solutions directly address the committee's focus areas.
4
Form effective counter-arguments — prepare responses to positions other delegates are likely to take.
